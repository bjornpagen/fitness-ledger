# Drew Baye: Critical Evidence Audit

**Evidence current through August 26, 2026**  
**Primary-source layer:** [Drew_Baye_Book_Claims_Catalog.md](Drew_Baye_Book_Claims_Catalog.md)

## Executive conclusion

Drew Baye's system is a credible **minimal-dose, time-efficient way to resistance train**, but the books repeatedly turn “one hard set can work” into “one failure set produces the greatest possible result.” That stronger claim does not survive the evidence. The 2026 ACSM position stand synthesized 137 reviews and more than 30,000 participants: resistance training works across many forms, hypertrophy is enhanced by higher weekly volume (about 10 or more sets per muscle per week in the aggregate evidence), and neither failure, equipment type, nor time under tension consistently determines outcomes [R1]. A 67-study dose-response meta-regression independently found positive, diminishing-return relationships between weekly volume and hypertrophy/strength [R2].

The best parts to retain are controlled technique, progressive overload, accurate logs, simple equipment, compounds for efficiency, individualized exercise selection, adequate recovery, resistance training during fat loss, high protein, and skepticism toward unnecessary supplements. The parts to reject or sharply qualify are single-set maximality, routine absolute failure, universal ultralow frequency, very slow cadence as uniquely beneficial, resistance-circuit/aerobic equivalence, categorical anti-running claims, near-elimination of grains/legumes/vegetable oils, the 31.4-kcal-per-pound-of-fat rule as an individual ceiling, and ECA/yohimbine instructions.

Baye's position is not static. In 2012–2015 he says failure is useful but “not absolutely necessary”; in 2025 he claims one failure set gives the “greatest possible” gain. In 2019 he retracts the unilateral Kratos ladder and isochronal progression. In 2025 he retracts condensed pre-exhaustion, rest-pause, negative-only work, and routine set extension. His 2024–2025 push/pull/legs examples are explicitly tied to testosterone and other anabolic drugs and should not be generalized to a drug-free general-population program.

For the fixed policy, the evidence supports the Monday/Thursday full-body routine: two dynamic work sets on the first six exercises, one dynamic set on lower-back extension, abdominal flexion, and calf press, plus one 90-second TSC each for neck extension and neck flexion. Dynamic work uses 8–12 repetitions targeting about 2 RIR, ordinary controlled 2-1-2 repetitions, and two-minute rests. RIR, cadence and rest are practical standards, not uniquely optimal biological values. TSC is an equipment workaround rather than a uniquely superior training method, and its force cannot be quantified without measuring equipment. Dedicated aerobic work remains a distinct stimulus, supplied by the fixed Tuesday/Friday/Saturday e-bike sessions. Both modes start at 07:00. The routine is an effective sustainable dose; it does not claim that this volume maximizes every possible hypertrophy outcome.

The final 11 exercises are audited directly against Baye's own passages in [Exercise_Selection_Primary_Source_Audit.md](Exercise_Selection_Primary_Source_Audit.md). That audit supersedes the exercise-specific consequences in earlier drafts of this document.

## Method

All seven supplied PDFs were read page by page (602 PDF pages total). Text extraction was reliable; the 2012 cover is image-only and its PDF pp. 5, 53, 85, and 113 are intentionally blank. No unreadable content page was omitted. Printed/PDF mappings and 101 source propositions appear in the claims catalog. The previously prepared guide was read in full and treated as a hypothesis to audit rather than an authority.

Scientific judgments prioritize systematic reviews, meta-analyses, position stands, then randomized trials and mechanistic human work. Actual PubMed records/abstracts were inspected; search snippets were not used. “Supports” below means the strongest fair steelman, not proof. “Opposes” identifies the evidence that limits the claim. Population means do not guarantee an individual response, and a method can be effective without being necessary or optimal.

The 101 rows below use the required verdict vocabulary. Each supplies the claim and page, strongest support, strongest opposition, permitted conclusion and policy consequence, and confidence. Reference keys resolve to full titles and stable links at the end.

## Audit of the previous research guide

### What it captured accurately

The moved guide correctly identified Baye's recurring emphasis on effort, brief training, controlled repetitions, progression, machines as legitimate tools, resistance training during fat loss, calorie balance, relatively high protein, and skepticism of pre-workout static stretching. Its most useful high-level diagnosis also survives: Baye often turns an effective minimum into an unsupported optimum.

### What was missing or blended

- It lacked book page citations and blended external blogs/interviews with the supplied books.
- It compressed 101 narrower propositions into roughly 40, hiding distinctions among effective, sufficient, necessary and optimal.
- It missed Baye's 2013/2015 qualification that failure is “not absolutely necessary,” his 2019 retraction of unilateral Kratos and isochronal progression, and his 2025 reversals on pre-exhaustion/rest-pause/negative-only/set-extension work.
- It did not foreground that Baye's 2024–2025 PPL routines were enabled by testosterone/other anabolic drugs [Baye, *High Intensity Workouts 2025 Workouts Supplement*, PDF p. 33, printed p. 30].
- It overgeneralized his warm-up position: he does allow warm-ups for painful joints, heavy advanced work and rehearsal [Baye, *High Intensity Workouts*, 2nd ed., PDF pp. 67–68, printed pp. 63–64].
- It underdeveloped TSC's angle specificity, blood-pressure/pressor caution and poor measurability without a force meter.
- It missed the 2019 warning against hands-behind-torso bench dips, directly relevant to the available equipment.

### Verdicts revised

The prior guide treated a developed “Omega Set”/cross-bridge/microtrauma theory as book-derived. It is not in the supplied PDFs. Muscle damage as the primary growth target is scientifically weak [R23–R25], but the attribution must be to separate public material, not these books. The guide also called separate warm-up categorically contradicted; the better verdict is **PARTIALLY HOLDS** because low-load familiar machine work may need little preparation and the 2025 warm-muscle meta-analysis did not improve maximal force, though it did improve rate-dependent properties [R13]. Its mixed verdict on running was too generous for Baye's actual categorical wording: “does not improve” and “will eventually destroy” are **CONTRADICTED**.

## Strongest and weakest aspects of the philosophy

**Strongest:** simplicity; precise exercise/setup records; progressive overload; controlled technique; compound efficiency without pretending isolation is useless; equipment pluralism; explicit detraining exception to maximal effort; resistance training during energy restriction; calorie calibration; sensible protein/meal-frequency advice; later willingness to retract poor methods.

**Weakest:** converting sufficiency into maximality; treating failure as privileged; excluding volume from the stimulus; equating acute cardiometabolic stress with chronic aerobic adaptation; universal language about running, warm-ups and cadence; static mid-range work without adequate angle caveats; outdated stimulant instructions; nutritional exclusions unsupported by outcome evidence.

## Direct answers to the central questions

1. **What does Baye recommend?** A brief, low-frequency, usually single-set, failure-oriented system using controlled/very slow repetitions, progressive logs, compounds and flexible equipment, sometimes with short rests or TSC. For fat loss he retains resistance training, controls calories, raises protein and largely rejects cardio.
2. **What should be retained?** Technique, progression, logs, stable exercise selection, compounds for efficiency, individual fit, recovery, resistance training during dieting, protein and calorie calibration.
3. **Effective but not optimal?** One set, very low frequency, TSC, 60–90 s TUL, and many slow-cadence routines.
4. **Contradicted or obsolete?** Single-set maximality, routine failure superiority, cardio equivalence, categorical anti-running, proportional strength=size, exact fat-transfer ceiling, grain/legume/oil exclusion, and ECA/yohimbine advice.
5. **What changed?** Failure rhetoric strengthens by 2025 while most postfailure methods are retracted; Kratos unilateral/isochronal progressions are retracted in 2019; equipment/split options broaden; 2025 discloses drug-assisted PPL context.
6. **How should it be integrated?** Use Baye's simplicity and record keeping inside the fixed two-day, moderate-volume, subfailure strength program plus dedicated e-bike work.
7. **Gain versus fat loss lifting?** Exercise selection and technique stay nearly the same. In a deficit, progression expectations fall, fatigue may justify modest volume reduction, and preserving load/reps matters more than adding novelty.
8. **Is Baye right that lifting changes little between goals?** **Mostly, yes**, for the resistance protocol. He is wrong if that is extended to total programming: calorie intake, expected progress, aerobic activity and recovery management change materially.
9. **What needs aerobic work?** Maximizing VO2max/VO2peak, sustained endurance, and central/peripheral aerobic adaptations such as stroke volume/plasma-volume and mitochondrial/capillary development. Lifting may improve some, but not equivalently [R16].
10. **What causal model explains the program?** Progressive high-tension contractions recruit/fatigue fibers and provide a graded stimulus; sufficient weekly sets accumulate that signal; recovery/protein supply remodeling; energy balance governs fat-mass direction; repeated aerobic demand drives distinct oxygen-delivery/utilization adaptations. The operating manual turns each link into a measurable error-correction rule.

5. **R5 — Robinson ZP, Pelland JC, Remmert JF, et al.** “Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy.” *Sports Medicine*. 2024. Closer proximity may favor hypertrophy, but the precise relationship remains uncertain. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2). Limitation: RIR often estimated retrospectively at study level.
6. **R6 — Refalo MC, Helms ER, Trexler ET, et al.** “Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine*. 2023. No clear hypertrophy superiority of momentary failure. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y). Limitation: limited number of well-controlled direct comparisons.
7. **R7 — Cowley N, Nicholson V, Timmins R, et al.** “The Effects of Advanced Resistance Training Prescription Methods on Strength, Power, Hypertrophy, and Performance Adaptations in Healthy Adults: A Systematic Review and Bayesian Network Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine*. 2026. No clear general advantage over traditional training. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41951916/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02428-1). Limitation: heterogeneous methods and sparse networks for individual techniques.
8. **R8 — Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn DI, Krieger JW.** “Effect of Repetition Duration During Resistance Training on Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine*. 2015. Broad repetition durations can grow muscle; very long reps appear inferior. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0304-0). Limitation: few trials and inconsistent tempo control.
9. **R9 — Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn D, Krieger JW.** “Effects of Resistance Training Frequency on Measures of Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine*. 2016. Twice-weekly muscle training outperformed once weekly in the available data. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8). Limitation: frequency is confounded with/distributes volume in parts of the literature.
10. **R10 — Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Ogborn D, et al.** “Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training.” *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*. 2017. Hypertrophy was similar across loads when effort was high; strength favored heavier loads. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200). Limitation: many small, short trials.
11. **R11 — Singer A, Wolf M, Generoso L, et al.** “Give It a Rest: A Systematic Review with Bayesian Meta-analysis on the Effect of Inter-set Rest Interval Duration on Muscle Hypertrophy.” *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*. 2024. Small hypertrophy benefit above 60 seconds; little detected difference beyond roughly 90 seconds. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789). Limitation: sparse direct evidence for many rest-duration contrasts.
12. **R12 — Oranchuk DJ, Storey AG, Nelson AR, et al.** “Isometric Training and Long-term Adaptations: Effects of Muscle Length, Intensity, and Intent: A Systematic Review.” *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*. 2019. Isometrics work, with joint angle, muscle length, intensity and intent affecting transfer. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30580468/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13375). Limitation: heterogeneous and often small studies.
13. **R13 — Wilson CJ, Nunes JP, Blazevich AJ.** “The Effect of Muscle Warm-up on Voluntary and Evoked Force-time Parameters: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis with Meta-regression.” *Journal of Sport and Health Science*. 2025. Warm-up improved rate-dependent properties, not maximal force. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39864808/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2025.101024). Limitation: muscle warming is narrower than every real-world warm-up goal.
14. **R14 — Haugen ME, Vårvik FT, Larsen S, et al.** “Effect of Free-weight vs. Machine-based Strength Training on Maximal Strength, Hypertrophy and Jump Performance.” *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation*. 2023. Similar hypertrophy; strength gains were test/modality specific. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-023-00713-4). Limitation: limited studies and intervention durations.
15. **R15 — Lauersen JB, Bertelsen DM, Andersen LB.** “The Effectiveness of Exercise Interventions to Prevent Sports Injuries.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2014. Stretching did not reduce injuries; strength/balance/multicomponent exercise did. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24100287/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092538). Limitation: sports injury prevention is not identical to every gym population or chronic flexibility outcome.
16. **R16 — An J, Su Z, Meng S.** “Effect of Aerobic Training Versus Resistance Training for Improving Cardiorespiratory Fitness and Body Composition in Middle-aged to Older Adults.” *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics*. 2024. Aerobic training was superior for VO2max/6-minute walk; resistance training was superior for lean mass. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38878596/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.archger.2024.105530). Limitation: population is middle-aged/older and does not test every circuit protocol.
17. **R17 — Schumann M, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, et al.** “Compatibility of Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training for Skeletal Muscle Size and Function.” *Sports Medicine*. 2022. Concurrent training did not meaningfully impair hypertrophy/maximal strength; same-session work may impair explosive strength. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7). Limitation: protocols and training status vary.
18. **R18 — Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, et al.** “A Systematic Review, Meta-analysis and Meta-regression of Protein Supplementation on Resistance-training Gains.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2018. Average benefit plateaued near 1.6 g/kg/day, with upper uncertainty around 2.2 g/kg/day. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608). Limitation: supplementation studies, mostly healthy adults not deliberately dieting.
19. **R19 — Alpert SS.** “A Limit on the Energy Transfer Rate from the Human Fat Store in Hypophagia.” *Journal of Theoretical Biology*. 2005. The source of the approximate 31.4-kcal/lb-fat/day model. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15615615/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2004.08.029). Limitation: theoretical inference from Minnesota Starvation data, not a prospective individual prescription trial.
20. **R20 — Murphy C, Koehler K.** “Energy Deficiency Impairs Resistance Training Gains in Lean Mass but Not Strength.” *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*. 2022. Deficits impaired lean-mass gains; deficits over ~500 kcal/day prevented gains on average. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075). Limitation: meta-regression average, not an individual hard ceiling.
21. **R21 — Longland TM, Oikawa SY, Mitchell CJ, et al.** “Higher Compared with Lower Dietary Protein During an Energy Deficit Combined with Intense Exercise Promotes Greater Lean Mass Gain and Fat Mass Loss.” *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*. 2016. High-protein young men gained lean mass during a marked deficit/intense program. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.119339). Limitation: short, supervised study in young men; not a guarantee of true myofiber gain or generalizability.
22. **R22 — Kodama S, Saito K, Tanaka S, et al.** “Cardiorespiratory Fitness as a Quantitative Predictor of All-cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Events.” *JAMA*. 2009. Higher CRF strongly predicted lower mortality/CVD event risk. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19454641/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.681). Limitation: prognostic observational evidence does not by itself prove a specific training prescription.
23. **R23 — Damas F, Libardi CA, Ugrinowitsch C.** “The Development of Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy Through Resistance Training: The Role of Muscle Damage and Muscle Protein Synthesis.” *European Journal of Applied Physiology*. 2018. Damage is not a necessary mediator of hypertrophy. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29282529/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9). Limitation: narrative/mechanistic synthesis.
24. **R24 — Damas F, Phillips SM, Libardi CA, et al.** “Resistance Training-induced Changes in Integrated Myofibrillar Protein Synthesis Are Related to Hypertrophy Only After Attenuation of Muscle Damage.” *Journal of Physiology*. 2016. Early protein synthesis largely supported repair; later synthesis tracked hypertrophy after damage subsided. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP272472). Limitation: small longitudinal human study.
25. **R25 — Wackerhage H, Schoenfeld BJ, Hamilton DL, et al.** “Stimuli and Sensors that Initiate Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy Following Resistance Exercise.” *Journal of Applied Physiology*. 2019. Reviews mechanical/energetic signaling rather than a single damage trigger. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335577/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00685.2018). Limitation: mechanistic review cannot set an exact training dose.
26. **R26 — Aune D, Keum N, Giovannucci E, et al.** “Whole Grain Consumption and Risk of Cardiovascular Disease, Cancer, and All Cause and Cause Specific Mortality.” *BMJ*. 2016. Higher whole-grain intake associated with lower major-disease/mortality risk. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27301975/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716). Limitation: prospective observational evidence; healthy-user/confounding remains possible.
27. **R27 — Afshin A, Micha R, Khatibzadeh S, et al.** “Consumption of Nuts and Legumes and Risk of Incident Ischemic Heart Disease, Stroke, and Diabetes.” *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*. 2014. Legume/nut intake was not harmful and was associated with lower ischemic-heart-disease risk. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24898241/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.113.076901). Limitation: few trials and observational confounding.
28. **R28 — Marklund M, Wu JHY, Imamura F, et al.** “Biomarkers of Dietary Omega-6 Fatty Acids and Incident Cardiovascular Disease and Mortality.” *Circulation*. 2019. Higher linoleic-acid biomarkers were associated with lower CVD risk, not categorical harm. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30971107/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.038908). Limitation: biomarker cohort pooling is observational and does not address repeated deep-frying/oxidized oils.
29. **R29 — Shekelle PG, Hardy ML, Morton SC, et al.** “Efficacy and Safety of Ephedra and Ephedrine for Weight Loss and Athletic Performance.” *JAMA*. 2003. Modest short-term loss with increased adverse symptoms and inadequate long-term safety. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12672771/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.289.12.1537). Limitation: older trials and incomplete power for rare severe events.
30. **R30 — Cohen PA, Wang YH, Maller G, et al.** “Pharmaceutical Quantities of Yohimbine Found in Dietary Supplements in the USA.” *Drug Testing and Analysis*. 2016. Yohimbine amount and labeling varied markedly. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26391406/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/dta.1849). Limitation: product-content study, not an efficacy trial.
31. **R31 — Bouchard C, An P, Rice T, et al.** “Familial Aggregation of VO2max Response to Exercise Training: Results from the HERITAGE Family Study.” *Journal of Applied Physiology*. 1999. Demonstrated substantial familial variation in trainability. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10484570/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1999.87.3.1003). Limitation: does not make VO2max unimportant or fixed.
32. **R32 — Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ, Wildman R, et al.** “International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: Diets and Body Composition.” *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*. 2017. Fat loss is driven by a sustained deficit; many dietary patterns can work, with protein, adherence and individual preference shaping implementation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y). Limitation: position stand synthesizes heterogeneous diet research and is not an individualized clinical diet.
33. **R33 — Antonio J, Candow DG, Forbes SC, et al.** “Common Questions and Misconceptions About Creatine Supplementation: What Does the Scientific Evidence Really Show?” *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*. 2021. Reviews efficacy, dosing, water retention and safety evidence; loading is optional and maintenance dosing is effective. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33557850/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-021-00412-w). Limitation: narrative evidence review and not a substitute for individualized medical advice.

---

**Bottom line:** Baye supplies a useful behavioral shell—simple exercises, careful execution, progression and records. Current evidence supplies the missing dose logic: multiple quality sets can add benefit, failure is optional, aerobic work is a distinct stimulus, and the correct dose is found by measured response rather than HIT axioms.
